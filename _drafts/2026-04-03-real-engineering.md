I have been asked this question in some variation basically since the second year at my current job. Mainly not the people at my job (though some have), its mostly the people that I meet in my extracurriculars that ask me how I learned all the engineering things I know and why I'm not in a better position than I'm currently in. I truly am flattered to receive this question and praise that goes along with it, but I'll be the first to say I'm not as great an engineer (or even really an engineer at all) as outsiders see me. The point of this post is to break down where that question comes from, why it feels like stolen valor, and what I want to do to be a "real" engineer.

## What is engineering?

The term 'engineer' is defined as
> a person who designs, builds, or maintains machines, structures, or systems.

By definition, one could say I am an engineer. I have come up with and designed various little gadgets and programs in the past, I've done a lot of work with Arduino-supported dev boards and Raspberry Pis, currently daily driving Ubuntu and getting into embedded Linux, working in KiCAD to make boards for these devices, learning CAD for FDM 3D printing, so on and so forth; this post would be quite long if I listed everything I have have used or somewhat experimented with. I also actively provide some support for a couple different engineering groups like a robotics team and aerospace organization.

## Stolen Valor

Where I believe the confusion comes from is the way I present myself. There is an assumption that if I am a part of these groups and I can tell you generally how something works, I know everything about it or how to do everything related to it, which is NOT how that works.

A great example of this is with the OreSat 0.5 cubesat: Myself, a PSU student, and the faculty advisor spent ~2-3 hours one day assembling and documenting the process to making the solar modules for the satellite. It was a wonderful experience feeling like being a part of a real engineering team and making an actually helpful contribution, couldn't have asked for anything more. However, if you were to ask me how the solar system (pun not intended) for the satellite works, I wouldn't even know where to begin let alone anything beyond the basic "solar cells convert light to electricity". There's so much to the solar part of the satellite involving MPPT, charge management, not backdriving the cells, and more that I have no clue about. Do I want to learn about those things? Absolutely, it's super interesting to me and that knowledge would serve me well for a few personal projects using solar cells. But do I know anything about it now? Not in the slightest, not yet at least.

The thing I emphasize when I talk about these engineering teams boils down to "I know a little about a lot"; I am generally curious about how things are made in all aspects from the mechanical design to the electrical design to firmware development, and I try to involve myself in as many of those things as I can to learn as much as I can.

## A more accurate label and why it doesn't matter

If there were to be a more accurate label to describe my enthusiasm for tech and engineering, it would be just that: an enthusiast. Personally, I feel that I don't do enough meaningful, iterative work in these groups to have "earned" the title of engineer. "Being an engineer" has a certain weight and impact to it that it just feels wrong to be referred as one. Maybe I'm holding too much prestige to it, but that's just how I view it. In the grand scheme of things, the label doesn't matter (with the exception of job applications). It's more about the work that you do than the title of your job, which is more aligned with the resistance I have.

## How to be a real engineer

Being a "real engineer" to me implies that you can come up with a solution to a problem, be able to implement and test it, determine if it's "good enough", and iterate if it requires improvements.
